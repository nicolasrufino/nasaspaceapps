Martian Map · scouting system · NASA Space Apps 2026

Where to send the helicopter, and when to stop

Orbit maps miss what stops a walker: rock fields, soft sand, short ledges. Helicopter flights can check the ground, but they are scarce. Martian Map flies only where the answer could change the walking route, and stops when one more flight would not change the plan enough to pay for itself.

The goalBest route per flight spentNot the best route possible. That would need every cell scouted.
The theoryCanadian Traveller Problem with remote sensingKnown roads may be blocked; you can pay to check one first (Bnaya, Felner & Shimony, 2009).
Who decidesAn algorithm, not a neural networkMachine learning only improves what the algorithm believes about the ground.

      3 · What we train

      One model trained today, three planned for scouting

      Each model is graded on data it never saw before it is used. A model that does not beat the simpler rule is not shipped.

      1trained today (ground photos)
      3planned for scouting
      1optional (Moon pre-training)
      1later (learned policy)
      0models that make the decision
      Not machine learning, on purpose
      • ROSCO, NASA/JPL's rock detector: finds rocks by their shadows at a known sun angle. Already run on one Gale image; 23 rocks accepted by Jules.
      • Rock-size law (Golombek and colleagues): counts of big rocks predict how many small ones there are. Used for every Mars landing site.
      • Walking planner: least-energy route (Dijkstra) and best stop order (Held-Karp). About 0.1 s on the 256 × 256 Gale grid in a browser.
      • Value-of-information planner: the decision itself. Explainable in kilojoules: "this strip could save 4 kJ; nothing else is worth more."

      5 · The helicopter

      Adapted from SkyFall, recharged at the habitat

      JPL's SkyFall is the proof that scouting helicopters are planned. Its September 2026 mission package gives each helicopter about 1–2 km and 2.5 minutes per flight sol, then a sol on the ground to recharge. It does not give battery capacity; we fill that gap from flown and studied craft and label every gap-filling number as an estimate.

      CraftStatusWhat we take from it
      SkyFall (JPL)Launch 2028Three helicopters, mid-air deployment. Colour cameras pointing down and at the horizon, near-IR cameras, ground-penetrating radar, a dosimeter, air temperature. About 1–2 km and 2.5 min per flight sol, then a recharge sol. Fleet, sensors and flight cadence.
      IngenuityFlew 72 times, 2021–2024Longest flight 704 m, up to about 170 s. Flew around midday. Crashed over featureless sand. 700 m per flight (knob), daylight only, no-fly over smooth sand.
      Mars Science HelicopterNASA conceptAbout 30 kg, about 5 kg payload, roughly 10–12 km per flight; recharge over one sol (single source). Where range could go.
      Dragonfly (Titan)Launch 2028Recharges its battery between flights from a nuclear generator of about 100 W. Recharging from a base, not the Sun.
      Fission Surface PowerNASA project, early 2030s40 kW, designed to carry over to Mars. The power a crewed base could have. Our recharge assumption, not a NASA plan.

      How low should it fly?

      The idea: about 2 m, looking at a slant
      • Curiosity's navigation cameras sit 1.94–1.99 m above the wheels, so a rover-trained model would see a familiar view.
      • A helicopter never pays to back out of a dead end: it lifts over. That asymmetry is what makes scouting cheap.
      Why we propose above about 6 m, camera tilted
      • Ingenuity's rotor lifted dust below 3 m on take-off, from 2.3–3.6 m on landing, and at times at 5.1–5.7 m in flight (Lemmon et al., 2022).
      • Gale's boxwork ridges reach 1.7 m inside a cell (90th percentile), leaving about 0.3 m at 2 m.
      • Same height did not help rover-to-rover (93% → 48%). Match the slant, not the height, and test on Ingenuity's public colour images first.
