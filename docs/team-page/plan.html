What Martian Map is, and why it works

What Mission VESTA is, and why it works

Read this first. It explains the product in order: the challenge, our answer, each stage and why it exists, and why our answer beats the alternatives on every objective NASA set.

1. The challenge

NASA asks for a layered map of one place on Mars, built from several NASA missions, that helps astronauts plan and carry out a surface walk safely while doing new science. The map must:

  • combine data from several missions into one view (R1);
  • start from a science goal and a site, such as the boxwork ridges on Mount Sharp (R2);
  • layer at least three kinds of data (R3);
  • put safety first: space weather, what a suited person can carry and climb, a route around obstacles (R4);
  • work at the scale of one walk, not a region (R5).

2. The problem underneath

Two facts make this hard.

  1. No map is detailed enough for one walk. The map that covers almost all of Mars (the CTX mosaic) is 5 m per pixel. The sharpest images (HiRISE, 25 cm) cover only about 3 to 5% of the planet. Even HiRISE misses rocks smaller than about 1 m and cannot tell firm ground from soft sand. In NASA's 2022 field test in Arizona, crews had 1 m maps, and the first walk still hit slopes and drop-offs it could not cross and ended short.
  2. Earth cannot help in time. A message takes 4 to 22 minutes each way. Mission control cannot guide a walk step by step the way it does on the Space Station. The crew has to carry the plan with them and make the calls on site.

3. Our answer

Martian Map turns NASA's Mars data into a science walk you can plan, preview and come home from, and makes the map better with every walk.

What it is, concretely: one app, three views of the same map.

  • The habitat display (the sol before): pick a science goal, see the layers, send the scout helicopter (it flies in daylight), approve the route and the packing list.
  • The tablet the crew carries (during the walk): a minimap like in a video game: where you are, where to go, the path, and the layers that matter (ground, minerals, chemistry, radiation). A science checklist at each stop.
  • The helmet HUD (during the walk): only what matters right now: how far you can still go and get home, heading, the next stop, alerts.

In the demo a phone plays the tablet and switches to the HUD view.

Who uses it: a two-person crew on Mars, before, during and after a walk. Where we build it: one 2 km × 2 km slice of lower Mount Sharp in Gale crater (see docs/DATA.md).

4. The idea in one line: coarse to fine

Each stage sees more detail than the one before, covers less ground, and costs more. So each stage tells the next one where to look.

Orbit sees everything, coarsely, for free. The helicopter sees fine detail, but only along a few hundred metres per flight. The crew sees everything up close, but their walk time is the most precious thing on the mission. Martian Map spends each one where it matters most.

5. Each stage, and why it is there

Stage 1: Orbit. Start from what NASA already knows

  • What happens: the app loads NASA's orbital layers for the slice: images, elevation and slope, sand (from thermal data), minerals, the geologic map, radiation and weather measured by Curiosity.
  • Why start here: it is the only data that exists for the whole site before anyone lands, and it is already paid for. Nobody should plan a walk blind when orbiters have been mapping Mars for 20 years.
  • What it makes easier:
    • Where to go: minerals and geology point to the science (the boxwork ridges, places where water once flowed).
    • Where not to go: slopes too steep for a suit, sand fields, high-radiation exposure.
    • Where to look closer: it narrows 4 km² down to a few corridors, so the helicopter flies only where it matters.
  • Its limit, and why we don't stop here: orbit cannot see the small things that stop a person on foot (rocks under about 1 m, soft sand, short steep steps). The JETT3 test showed that plans built on orbit-scale maps break on contact.
  • Objectives it meets: R1 (several missions in one view), R2 (science goal to targets), R3 (several data types), R5 (one site, not a region).
  • In our demo: the "orbit view" is a coarse version of the slice, about the detail most of Mars has.

Stage 2: Scout. A helicopter looks before people walk

  • What happens: in daylight on the sol before the walk, a small scout helicopter flies the planned route first: the kind NASA JPL flew on Mars (Ingenuity) and is now having AeroVironment build for crew scouting (SkyFall, launch late 2028). It covers the route corridor first, then anomalies (places where the layers disagree, such as firm-looking ground that the thermal data says is loose sand, a slope near the 20° limit, or an unusual mineral signal near a target), then the cells it is least sure about. It avoids smooth sand, where camera navigation fails (what ended Ingenuity). Where it flies, the map gains centimetre detail: the terrain and the hazards below orbit's limit (rocks under about 1.5 m, half-metre drops). The route re-plans around what it found.
  • Why a scout at all: walk time is the scarcest resource. Finding a blocked path halfway through a walk costs oxygen, energy and maybe the walk. Finding it from the habitat costs a 3-minute flight.
  • Why a helicopter and not a rover: an Ingenuity-class helicopter covers about 700 m in 3 minutes. A rover on autonomous driving averages about 110 m an hour.
  • Why we believe it works: Ingenuity flew 72 times on Mars as "an aerial scout" for Perseverance's drivers; its Flight 12 images confirmed the rover's route into South Séítah was drivable. NASA's SkyFall (three helicopters, contracted, launch late 2028) will scout exploration zones for crews, and MoonFall's four hopping drones will survey Artemis sites before astronauts arrive. Every link, every limit and our answers: docs/RESEARCH.md §14.
  • What it makes easier: surprises move from the walk to the planning table.
  • Objectives it meets: R4 (route around obstacles), C6 (robots supporting the crew).
  • How it sees in 3D: the same idea as a bat's echolocation, but with light instead of sound, because Mars' thin air loses high-pitched sound within metres. The main sensor is cameras: overlapping photos turned into 3D, already done with Ingenuity's images at 5 cm detail. A laser adds exact height; radar could later show what is underneath. Details in docs/RESEARCH.md §13.
  • In our demo: the flight is simulated; what it reveals is real NASA data, the full-resolution images and elevation of the same slice, labelled "simulated helicopter view, real HiRISE data". As it flies, a scan sweep shows rays hitting the terrain and dots building up before the full detail fades in.

Stage 3: Plan and pack. Decide before you leave

  • What happens: Martian Map plans the best route (safe and efficient, not shortest) to the science stops. For every stretch it estimates effort, time, oxygen and radiation dose, and where the point of no return is. It shows rover photos of each stop and builds the packing list: what to bring, how heavy it is, what the buddy rover carries.
  • Why plan before leaving: with Earth up to 22 minutes away, the plan has to be made ahead and carried by the crew. NASA plans every walk today the same way, with limits like "always able to get home in one hour".
  • Why the packing list matters: weight changes how much energy each step costs. In NASA's 2022 test, pulling a loaded cart caused unacceptable fatigue. So the route decides the load, and the load changes the route.
  • What it makes easier: the crew approves a plan in minutes, knowing its limits before the hatch opens, instead of building it by hand.
  • Objectives it meets: R4 (suit mobility and loads, radiation, safe route), C5 (rover photos of each stop), C1 (habitat display).

Stage 4: Walk and add. Guide the walk, and learn from it

  • What happens: on the suit display, real steps move the astronaut along the route. A ring on the map shows how far they can still go and get home, and it shrinks with every step. Past the point of no return, it alerts. At each stop, a short checklist: look, get close, decide, sample or move on. Along the way the map grows: photos, ground type (read by our classifier), how hard each stretch really was, markers and sample sites.
  • Why guide on the device: Earth can't do it in time, so the safety check has to run with the crew.
  • Why add to the map: the plan is a prediction; the walk is a measurement. The crew is the most detailed sensor on Mars, and every step they take confirms or corrects the map.
  • What it makes easier: the crew focuses on science while the app watches the margin home.
  • Objectives it meets: R4 (safety during the walk), R2 (the science at each stop), C2 (a tool that makes maps, not a static one).

Stage 5: Next walk. Start from a better map

  • What happens: everything from the walk (corrected terrain, real effort, new hazards, samples taken) goes back into the site's map. The next plan starts from it.
  • Why: each walk makes the next one safer and lets it go further, because less margin is spent on what nobody knew.
  • What it makes easier: walks get longer and more productive over the mission, and the same tool trains crews on Earth before they go.
  • Objectives it meets: C2 (a map-making tool), C3 (learning from maps and field tests).

7. What is real and what is simulated

  • Real: every data layer (NASA orbiters and Curiosity), the full-detail view the helicopter reveals, the walking-energy model, the route and safety maths, the classifier's training labels.
  • Simulated: the helicopter's flight, the astronaut (a judge walking with a phone), and the delay to mission control.
  • Not claimed: that a phone is the suit display (it stands in for one), or numbers we haven't measured. Every number in the pitch comes from our code.

Why nobody waits

  1. A fake world already exists. data/worlds/synthetic/ has every world file: height, slope, ground type, walking cost, cost home from every square, rocks, targets, stops and a route. Everyone builds against it from 9:00 on Saturday.
  2. Real data swaps in later through the same files. When Person 3 finishes the real Gale slice, the app and the models load it without code changes. Those files are the contract (docs/CONTRACTS.md).
  3. Placeholder models exist from hour one. Person 1 exports shape-correct ONNX files first, so the app can load "a model" before any model is trained.
  4. Every feature sits behind a flag. If something isn't ready by the freeze, it is switched off and the demo still runs.

Two worries, answered

  • "Turning the map into 3D will take a while." It won't. The 3D view is one flat mesh pushed up by the height of each square: about an hour in three.js, and it works on the fake world right away. It is only for people to picture the ground. All route maths runs on the flat grid.
  • "Analysing the map comes after the 3D." It doesn't wait for it. The analysis (walking cost, cost home from every square, the route) already runs in data/pipeline/make_world.py, on the fake world today and on the real slice as soon as it is cut.

The real long jobs are cutting and layering the real slice (Person 3), rock detection on real images (Person 1), and the walk itself on a phone (Person 2). They all start at 9:00, in parallel.

The NASA resources, explained

Our slice of Mars

We do not simulate the whole planet. We build one slice: about 2 km × 2 km of lower Mount Sharp, in Gale crater, along the stretch where Curiosity studied the boxwork ridges in 2025.

  • Why this slice:
    • NASA's challenge uses it as its example.
    • NASA's resource list includes Curiosity's route map of this area.
    • USGS already publishes detailed terrain and images for Curiosity's area.
    • Curiosity measured the radiation and weather here and photographed the ground.
  • Two resolutions of the same box:
    • A coarse "orbit view" of about 8 m per cell (256 × 256 cells): the map before the helicopter flies.
    • The full 1 m terrain and 25 cm images: what the helicopter "reveals".
  • What the box must contain (Person 3 picks it in Mars Trek):
    • boxwork ridges (the science);
    • at least one sand patch and one slope over 15° (the hazards);
    • a rock field that is hard to see at 8 m (the helicopter's moment).

The box we chose (D1, done)

  • Centre −4.800° N, 137.383° E; 2048 × 2048 m (lat −4.8173..−4.7827, lon 137.3657..137.4003), equirectangular Mars sphere (r = 3,396,190 m), same grid as the USGS mosaics. Full georeferencing in data/raw/gale/slice.json (made by data/pipeline/cut_slice.py).
  • Why here: it holds Curiosity's 2025 boxwork route (sols ~4480–4900: the Altadena, Valle de la Luna and Nevado Sajama drill sites, the ramp and the overlook), buttes with slopes over 25°, dark sand in the hollows, and blocky ground that only shows at 1 m.
  • Files: USGS 1 m DEM (MSL_Gale_DEM_Mosaic_1m_v3.tif) and 25 cm HiRISE colour (MSL_Gale_HiRISE-LRGB_78quads_sharp_cog.tif); the lossless greyscale 25 cm mosaic also works (--ortho gray, ~1.5 GB read). Only the box is read; nothing large goes in Git.

This is why it is a tool and not a static map: the challenge allows "a tool or system that generates maps", and every walk makes the map better.

Why the walks happen at all. Each walk has a science purpose. At Mount Sharp the boxwork ridges likely formed where groundwater flowed and left minerals behind (NASA's working idea, still being tested), the kind of place where signs of past habitability, and possibly life, are preserved. The map helps the crew get the most science per walk: which ridges to examine, which to sample, and what to leave for the next walk.

Who uses it: a two-person Mars crew, the night before and during a surface walk, with Earth 4 to 22 minutes away.

Four modes, one mission (the same steps Perseverance follows: choose a target, travel safely, investigate, decide what to keep):

Mode Where What happens
1. Plan Habitat display Pick a science goal. Layers highlight candidate targets. The scout helicopter flies the route and the map updates. Martian Map plans a safe, efficient route, estimates time, energy, oxygen and radiation, shows rover photos of every stop, and builds the packing list. The crew approves or edits.
2. Walk Phone as the suit display Real steps move you along the route. The walk-back ring shows how far you can still go and get home. The buddy rover carries the heavy kit and updates the local map. Hazards and space-weather alerts appear when they matter. Every stretch walked is added to the map.
3. At the stop Phone A short science checklist: look, get close, decide, sample or move on. Each sample gets a context card: place, layers, photo, time. Examine many targets, sample few.
4. Debrief Habitat display Replay the walk; predicted versus measured; what the crew saw updates the map for the next walk.

Who decides what (taken from how Perseverance works): people choose the destinations; the system chooses how to get there safely; at each stop the astronaut decides what science to do. On Mars nobody on Earth can make these calls in time, so the crew is the science team on site.

Training, too. Months before, crews rehearse the same walk on Earth: in the game on real terrain, and in an analog field site with the phone in walk mode, while instructors inject failures (a dust storm, a suit leak that cuts the budget). Every logged walk improves the walking-energy model.

Is a phone realistic? It stands in for the suit display (NASA's SUITS program builds helmet displays). NASA has let astronauts carry their own smartphones since February 2026 (Crew-12, Artemis II). With no GPS on Mars, position comes from counting steps and heading on the map, which is exactly what walk mode does.

"Optimal" means safe and efficient, not shortest. A straight line may cross loose sand or a slope a suited astronaut can't climb. A longer route that stays on firm ground and keeps the way home open is the better route, and the map shows why.

5. A Marswalk with Martian Map

  1. The night before (Plan). The crew picks "boxwork". Spectral and geology layers highlight three ridges. Martian Map plans a route: it avoids a sand patch (low thermal inertia) and a slope over the 20° limit, and takes a longer, firmer path. It shows Curiosity's photo of each stop, the walk time, energy, oxygen, dose, and the walk-back limit at every point. The crew moves one stop and approves.
  2. Walking out (Walk). The phone is the suit display. Steps and heading move the astronaut along the route. The buddy rover drives ahead the way Perseverance's AutoNav does: observe, map, evaluate, choose, move, update. It flags a rock field the orbital images missed, and the route updates. The ring shrinks with each step.
  3. At the ridge (At the stop). Look, get close, decide. The astronaut photographs the ridge; the classifier tags the ground; the astronaut samples one of three rocks examined, and the context card saves where, what layers said, and the photo.
  4. A solar storm alert. Minutes to shelter appear: the rover is 7 minutes away, a ridge wall 12.
  5. Turning back. The next target is past the point of no return. The phone buzzes. Mission control's reply arrives 20 seconds later (standing for 20 minutes): too late to help, which is the point.
  6. Debrief. Predicted versus measured energy, what the crew found, and the corrected map for the next walk.

6. Safety, in detail

  • Walk-back limit: if the rover fails, can the crew walk home with the reserve intact? Computed for every cell, along real routes and slopes. The reserve stands for NASA's 1-hour emergency return plus about 30 minutes to get back inside.
  • Ride-back limit: if a suit degrades, can the rover bring the astronaut home? Shown as a second, larger ring. Failures are not stacked, the same rule NASA uses.
  • Obstacles: slope limit, large rocks, loose sand; plans assume the map is wrong in places, so the route recomputes the moment the crew deviates.
  • Radiation: background dose for the walk and the shelter alert during a solar event.
  • Comms: delay to Earth and line of sight to the rover and habitat; everything works offline.
  • Wind is shown only as dust and visibility: on Mars it barely pushes.

7. Robots and pairs

  • The scout helicopter flies before the crew does, the job Ingenuity proved for Perseverance. It maps the route at centimetre scale and finds what orbit can't (rock fields, drop-offs, soft sand). In our app the flight is simulated over the real site, revealing a hidden fine-scale hazard layer; the route re-plans when it lands.
  • The buddy rover leads by default (crews in NASA's 2022 desert test preferred it), scouts the route, carries tools and samples, relays comms behind ridges, and can bring an astronaut home. It is specified like NASA's new Lunar Terrain Vehicle (km/h, with or without crew), because today's Mars rovers are 20–30 times slower than a walking astronaut.
  • Two astronauts walk together, in sight of each other, as NASA's EVA rules require. The map shows both and the ring follows the more tired one.
  • Override ladder: E-STOP, hold, come to me, return to base along the path it came. On lost link it stops, then retreats.

8. Why NASA cares

  • NASA's 2026 technology gaps rank surface mobility #2 of 32 and robots working with crew #10, and ask for "in-situ decision support tools" for Mars spacewalk hazards (need 8.06).
  • NASA is funding the rover (Lunar Terrain Vehicle, up to $4.6B) and the suit (orders capped at $3.1B). The layer between them, which tells the person in the suit what they can still do, is not funded; the one follow-me astronaut robot project is on hold.
  • Mars orbital support is shrinking, so crews will need onboard, offline tools.
  • The same engine works on the Moon (first crewed landing now planned for 2028) and for people on Earth who walk far from help: firefighters on steep ground, search and rescue, backcountry guides, polar expeditions.
  • For our company: a working demo of layered maps, terrain-aware routing, on-device ML and an AI agent that respects real limits.

Mission VESTA: Vesta Scout — five-minute pitch

Current script for F10, 2026-11-15. The accepted rescope governs the submission. Slides are in deck/. Every numerical product claim below comes from R5 and its JSON; these are fixed-world simulations, not field safety validation.

Scope and evidence

The judged path is bare /scout/, Gale, five default stops and the worst suit scenario. Budget: 3,165.2 kJ, with 1,899.1 kJ reserved. A scout campaign has at most three flights, each at most 1 km, and re-plans after every flight. A flight animates in ten seconds; this is presentation time, not aircraft performance. Repositioning, return-to-base flight, recharge, daylight and helicopter energy are not modelled.

The map uses HiRISE imagery and USGS terrain, with slope, crossing, rock, energy and science views. The route and return margin use the repository energy model, including assumptions about suit effort; they are not a certified safety guarantee. The imagery reveals existing NASA data, not newly acquired helicopter photographs. The ground-photo classifier and reviewed rock proposals are separate evidence, not the source of an aerial accuracy claim.

Script and slide order

1–3. One science walk and a way home (0:00–0:45)

A science walk is more than a line on a map. We need to know which stops we can reach, what might block the route, and whether we can still get home. Mission VESTA brings those questions together on one real patch of Gale crater. Vesta Scout is the planning map we can show today.

4–6. Plan, scout, re-plan, walk (0:45–1:30)

We start with NASA imagery and terrain. We select five science stops and plan a walking route with a return reserve. Three simulated helicopter flights inspect the route. Each landing adds observations and triggers a new plan. On the walk, the remaining return margin updates with movement. The map, flight and route are visible, so the crew can inspect what changed.

7. Live demo (1:30–3:00)

  1. Open bare /scout/ and leave the five default stops selected. Confirm Gale, the initial route near 621 kJ, HiRISE credit and terrain imagery. Use the Hazards view to explain the legend: slope in degrees, blocked crossings and rock evidence; return to Imagery for the flight.

  4. Switch to Walk, use Step, and point to the changing energy and return margin. Physical phone step sensing must be rehearsed on the actual phone before using it on stage; manual Step is the tested fallback. Keep the demo on the features visible in this build.
  5. If the live demo is unavailable, use slide 7's real screenshot and explicitly call it a recorded result.

8–9. NASA sources and measured proof (3:00–3:45)

HiRISE and the USGS terrain model provide the map. THEMIS and CRISM add orbital context; Curiosity supplies the science stops and photographs. In this fixed simulation, the initial route crosses six distinct cells from the stored hazard layer. After three flights, the final route crosses zero, with the same five stops. Walking energy increases from 621.4 to 630.5 kJ, or 1.46 percent. Even if walking costs are 30 percent higher, the model retains 446.5 kJ beyond the return cost and reserve.

Speaker detail: flights are 1,000 m + 1,000 m + 215.4 m, or 2.22 km of scanned corridor. The route changes from 1,274.7 m to 1,311.4 m. Nominal minimum spare margin: 635.6 kJ. The 44 found cells are across all scanned strips; the original route makes eight visits to six distinct hazard cells, and all six are eventually observed. The initial route crosses zero reviewed-rock-clearance cells: do not call these six cells six hand-checked rocks. Fully scanned means covered by this simulation's sensor footprint, not verified safe on Mars.

10–11. Why the comparison matters (3:45–4:25)

We compare the same world and the same five science stops. The result is a slightly longer, more demanding walk that avoids the hazards in the stored layer. We can reproduce the numbers, inspect the observations and see the cost of the detour. That is the useful result today: a decision the crew can examine, with the assumptions visible.

Judge questions

Question Answer supported by this build
What would the helicopter carry? NASA's SkyFall payload: two colour cameras, two near-infrared cameras, ground-penetrating radar, air-temperature sensors, a dosimeter and navigation cameras (NASA, Sept 2026). Six of the challenge's eight data kinds fly. Contact chemistry does not, so the crew measures it at the stop. In our build the sensors are not simulated.
How do you choose science targets? The goal is the boxwork. A ridge-and-hollow score from the 1 m terrain separates rover positions inside the boxwork from those outside at 0.851 on 90 cells (ml/exports/science_probe.json). It is a guide on a small test; the crew decides.
Are the flights real? No. A three-flight corridor simulation reveals a pre-existing hazard layer. Each flight is capped at 1 km. Aircraft transit, recharge, daylight and energy remain unmodelled.

Where did the hazard layer come from? Fine-terrain rules plus registered, reviewed ROSCO rock proposals. Proposal acceptance is not detector precision or recall. The measured route benefit here comes from terrain-derived hazards.
Does the classifier work from a helicopter? We have separate Curiosity ground-image tests. They do not establish aerial transfer. The assisted Ingenuity annotations are not an independent blind accuracy test.

Can I use the phone? The app has a Walk view and Step control. Physical device sensing and HTTPS rehearsal belong to the release/phone checklist, not to desktop browser verification.

Index of cell (x, y): y * width + x. World position of a cell centre: x * cell_size_m, y * cell_size_m.

The walk-back ring is every cell where cost_to_go[cell] <= energy_left - reserve, with reserve = reserve_fraction * energy_budget_j. The app only looks this up. No pathfinding runs on the phone.

Point of no return: the moment cost_to_go[current_cell] > energy_left - reserve. The phone buzzes.

Oxygen: the app converts energy to oxygen with kj_per_l_o2 in world.json (about 20 kJ per litre of oxygen), so the HUD can show oxygen left. Energy is the common driver of oxygen, CO2 and heat.

Reserve: reserve_fraction stands for NASA's 1-hour emergency return plus about 30 minutes of dust removal and getting back inside.

Energy scenarios (optional): a world may list "scenarios": {id: {"name", "energy_budget_j", "reserve_fraction", ...}} and "default_scenario"; energy_budget_j and reserve_fraction at the top level equal the default. The app switches with ?scenario=<id>. Gale: walking energy = (suit hours left − time at stops) × 1200 BTU/hr (NASA planetary suit sizing rate), reserve = 1.5 h at that rate; ideal = 8 h of suit, worst (default) = 5 h left after 3 h of station work.

Energy model: Pandolf (1977) metabolic rate with a terrain factor per class (knobs.eta, one per class id), times knobs.gravity_energy_factor (0.68: measured walking oxygen use at 0.38 g relative to Earth, an estimate to verify; replaces plain 0.38 gravity scaling). Sand's factor is set so flat sand costs knobs.sand_cost_ratio (2.4, Lejeune et al. 1998: 2.1-2.7x) times flat bedrock. The app reads these knobs; worlds without them use the old values. It is a simple first version, marked in the code. The planned upgrade is NASA JSC's field-modified model (Kim et al. 2024).

2. How NASA plans a surface walk today

  • Artemis rules (2023 "Tenets"): at most 2 km from the lander, sized so the crew can get back in 1 hour on contingency oxygen; communications may cut the range to 500 m–2 km and live video to about 300 m; every EVA keeps a reserve, so planned EVAs may be shorter than 8 hours. (NTRS)
  • How Artemis EVAs are planned: ground teams group science targets into EVAs within limits set by flight operations (maximum distance, maximum slope, maximum time without line of sight, consumables redlines). The crew gets a map book (slope, lighting, comm coverage), a cuff checklist, procedures and flight rules. The mapping tool is AEGIS, the Artemis EVA Geographic Information System. (NTRS 2025)
  • Mars (DRA 5.0): one habitat, two small pressurized rovers, two unpressurized rovers; about 20 km of walking per backpack charge; EVA teams of 2–4. With one rover the crew must stay within walkback or rescue range; with two, each rover can rescue the other. Regions of interest lie within about 100 km of the landing site. (NASA SP-2009-566, NTRS 2018)
  • Mars with delay (BASALT analog): 5- or 15-minute one-way delay, EVAs of about 4 hours, timelines with flexible buffers, the ground ranked samples on "leaderboards", and the crew had final authority; the ground only advised. BASALT used the Playbook tool, which re-projects the timeline from how long phases actually took. (PMC, PMC)

Where these rules came from (Apollo, 1969–72). The walkback limit (if the rover fails, walk home on what is left) and the rideback limit (if the backpack fails, ride home on the emergency system) were first set for Apollo, with emergency routes "planned with the same care as the nominal traverses" (NTRS 1970). Apollo 14–17 also carried a buddy hose to share cooling water. Artemis keeps the same idea with modern numbers.

3. The terrain nobody has seen up close

  • Orbit cannot see everything: the Moon's best camera (LROC, about 0.5 m per pixel) reliably shows boulders of about 1 m and larger; Mars' HiRISE resolves 25–32 cm per pixel. (NTRS)
  • Plans break on contact: in NASA's JETT3 field test (2022), maps matched what Artemis will have (1 m images, 1.5 m elevation). EVA 1 hit slopes over 16° and impassable drop-offs and ended short; EVAs 3 and 4 were replanned and shortened. Crews were told the planned path was a guide, not mandatory. (NTRS JETT3)
  • Scouting first: NASA Ames' "robotic recon" drives a rover over planned routes before the EVA to check hazards and rank targets; K10 rovers drove 45 km, mostly autonomously, at Haughton Crater in 2008. (CMU/Ames) At Black Point Lava Flow, a remotely driven rover scouted routes, the routes were revised, then two-person crews drove them (Fong, Bualat et al.).

5. What runs out first

  • EVA time is limited by oxygen, CO2 removal, cooling water, battery power and crew fatigue. NASA's own range table assumes about 2 km/h on level ground and ignores slope. (NASA 2020)
  • All of them follow effort. Oxygen used, CO2 made and heat to reject all scale with metabolic rate (about 20 kJ of work per litre of oxygen). That is why our ring is computed from walking energy: it is the common driver of every consumable.
  • Mars changes cooling: Apollo's sublimator needs a vacuum and is "infeasible on the Mars surface" (about 4.6 Torr); a water-membrane evaporator still worked up to 10 Torr. A Mars backpack sized for an 8-hour EVA: about 88 kg of hardware, 8.6 kg of oxygen, 2.9 kg of water. (NTRS ICES 2026, NTRS 2025)

6. Who carries what

  • Apollo: the handcart carried about 160 kg of tools and samples (Apollo 14 crews sometimes lifted it because that was faster); the rover carried 490 kg. (NASM, NSSDC)
  • JETT3: pushing a loaded cart caused unacceptable fatigue, so tools came off it; and when comms got poor, one crew member became a stationary relay and did no science.
  • DRA 5.0: move navigation, long-range comms, tools and spare consumables from the suit to the rover; rovers reduce fatigue and save consumables.

9. Who controls the robot, and what happens when things go wrong

  • The Moon allows remote driving; Mars does not. Moon round trip is about 2.6 s (in LTV tests, 4–8 s of delay already slowed drivers and raised workload). Mars round trip is 6–44 minutes, so control has to be by the crew nearby or by the robot itself. NTRS

13. How a scout helicopter sees in 3D

Echolocation: how bats do it

A bat makes a short, very high-pitched call (roughly 20 to 120 kHz, above human hearing) and listens for the echo.

  • Distance: the time until the echo comes back. Distance = speed of sound × time ÷ 2 (the sound goes there and back).
  • Direction: two ears. The echo reaches one ear slightly earlier and louder than the other.
  • Shape and motion: many calls per second, and changes in the echo's pitch, build a moving 3D picture.

Dolphins, submarine sonar, car parking sensors and some indoor drones use the same idea.

Why sound does not work on Mars

  • The air is too thin. Mars' air is about 1% as dense as Earth's, so sounds are about 20 dB quieter. (Nature 2022)
  • High pitches die within metres. Carbon dioxide absorbs high frequencies. A sound that carries about 65 m on Earth is lost after about 8 m on Mars, and high pitches are gone at that distance. (DLR) Bat-style ultrasound is the worst case.
  • There are two speeds of sound. Sound travels about 240 m/s, and about 10 m/s faster above roughly 400 Hz, which blurs the timing that echolocation depends on. (Nature 2022)
  • The helicopter drowns its own echo. Ingenuity's rotors spin about 2,400 rpm, right next to any microphone.

Verdict: echolocation by sound cannot map terrain from a Mars helicopter. The idea is exactly right, though. We only change what the helicopter sends out.

The same idea with light: lidar

Send laser pulses instead of sound and time the echo. Light needs no air, so this works on Mars, and it works in shadow because the helicopter brings its own light.

  • Caves on Earth are mapped this way. In DARPA's Subterranean Challenge (2018–2021), robots mapped caves, tunnels and buildings with no GPS, and almost every team built its maps around lidar. NASA JPL's team CoSTAR won the Urban round in 2020. (arXiv survey, NASA) The method is called SLAM: build the map and track your own position in it at the same time.
  • Ingenuity already flew one beam of it. A Garmin LIDAR-Lite v3 laser rangefinder pointed down and measured height 50 times a second. It was chosen because it was very light. (Garmin)
  • Its limit: a full scanning lidar is heavier and uses more power, and weight is the hardest limit on a Mars helicopter.

The same idea with radio: radar

Radio echoes pass through the ground. Ground-penetrating radar shows what is underneath: buried ice, hollows, and how deep loose sand goes. NASA's SkyFall mission (three helicopters, announced March 2026, launch late 2028) will carry cameras and ground-penetrating radar to scout exploration zones for crews and map ice. (Space.com) It answers "is it solid underneath?", not "where are the rocks?"

No echo at all: cameras (photogrammetry)

Take overlapping photos from different positions. Software matches the same points across photos and works out their 3D positions. This is called structure from motion, and it is how most drone maps on Earth are made.

  • It has already been done on Mars. A JPL study turned about 200 navigation-camera images from Ingenuity's Flight 12 into a terrain model with 5 cm spacing. (JPL poster)
  • At scale: a 2025 study processed 14,553 Ingenuity images into one map, with about 3 to 6 cm of height error from 10 m up, tied to HiRISE for position. (LPSC 2025)
  • Its limits: it needs daylight and texture, and it needs an orbital map (HiRISE) to place itself, because Mars has no GPS.

What a real scout would carry: cameras as the main 3D sensor (lightest, already proven), a laser for exact height and shadowed spots, and radar later for what lies underneath.

How our demo shows it

We do not build a sensor. We show what the sensor does, honestly:

  1. As the helicopter flies, it casts a fan of rays down onto the terrain, like a lidar sweep: "a bat, but with light".
  2. Where a ray hits the real 1 m terrain of our slice, a dot appears. The dots build up into a point cloud.
  3. Once a stretch is covered, the full-detail terrain and image fade in, the rocks appear, and the route re-plans.

Labelled: "simulated scan, real NASA terrain". The flight and the sweep are simulated; the terrain they reveal is the real USGS/HiRISE data. In three.js this is a raycaster against a hidden full-detail mesh, about 50 lines behind the helicopter_scout flag.

14. Can the scout helicopter actually work?

Checked Nov 14, 2026, because the whole pitch leans on it.

Verdict: yes, as long as we claim the right job. The helicopter's job is the hazards smaller than orbit can see: rocks under about 1.5 m, half-metre drops, the inside of a small crater. It flies in daylight on the sol before the walk. It is not "orbit is blurry". HiRISE already gives NASA slopes and big boulders, and a NASA judge will know that.

What this means for our build (proposal: needs P1 and P3 to agree, because it changes a contract)

Our current design has one weak spot. The "before" map is an 8 m version of the slice, and the "after" is HiRISE. HiRISE is also from orbit, so a judge can say "you already had that". Proposed fix:

  1. Before = everything HiRISE knows. Slopes from the 1 m terrain and rocks of 1.5 m and up go into cost_to_go before the helicopter flies. The 8 m grid stays, because it keeps the app fast on a phone, not because orbit is blurry.
  2. rocks_fine = hazards below HiRISE's limit.
    • Along the corridor: rocks of 0.5–1.5 m modelled from the HiRISE rock abundance with the size-frequency model NASA used for landing sites (Golombek), labelled "modelled".
    • At Curiosity's real boxwork stops: the actual ground. The rover's positions through sol 4865 come from the localized positions CSV (341 KB). Its top-down Navcam mosaics (vertical "VRT" projection) for sols 4534–4843 are in the MSL mosaic archive. They are PDS .IMG files; GDAL converts them. Test sol 4600 first to check alignment.
  3. The scout plan skips smooth sand.
  4. Labels: "simulated flight · HiRISE (orbit) and Curiosity Navcam (ground) are real · small rocks modelled from HiRISE rock abundance".
  5. R5 measures the gap: hazards below orbit's limit that the scouted route avoids. No published study has measured what a pre-EVA scout saves, so this is a first estimate. Label it as simulated.

Not worth it: moving to Jezero for real Ingenuity data. No Ingenuity terrain model is downloadable, only raw images, and we would lose the boxwork and Curiosity's instruments.
